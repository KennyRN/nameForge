# Nautical and hyperspace lexicon – review

A for-and-against pass over the two pasted notes, the way the stellar lexicon review handled the last set. **The lexicon** is the long structured word list (real nautical terms, motion terms, fluid dynamics, invented hyperspace terrain, a naming hierarchy, patterns). **The critique** is the shorter assessment of it (what works, pushback, the four chart quantities, compressibility, additions, three vocabularies).

For each claim: what holds up, what doesn't, and a revised verdict ready to become nameForge data. Where both notes share an assumption, it is tested once.

**Verdict key** (kept close to the stellar review's, so the two lexicons read alike):

- **Core**: everyday sailors' or spacers' generic – high weight, compounds freely.
- **Chart**: the formal form a chart prints – medium weight.
- **Survey**: specialist or theoretical – low weight; in hyperspace, the survey register only.
- **Civic**: built or settled – belongs in buildings and landmarks, or spacecraft and stations.
- **Route**: belongs in route names.
- **State**: a tidal or weather state – a modifier, never a generic.
- **Cut**: dropped.

**Scope flags**: **E** earthly seas, coasts and charts · **H** hyperspace charts.

## 1. The framing claims

### 1.1 'Terrain can be persistent behaviour, not only objects' (both)

**For:**
- Real oceans already work this way, which is the strongest argument either note could have made and neither does. The **Sargasso Sea** has no coast at all: it is bounded entirely by currents of the North Atlantic gyre. The **Doldrums**, **Horse Latitudes** and **Roaring Forties** are named belts of wind behaviour. The **Gulf Stream** and the **Kuroshio** are named currents; the **Great Whirl** off Somalia is a named eddy; Moskstraumen (the original *maelstrom*) and Corryvreckan are named whirlpools; the **Merry Men of Mey** is a named tidal race in the Pentland Firth; the **North Water** is a named polynya, an area kept free of ice by wind and current.
- Surf breaks are named persistent waves at fixed places, so even waves can earn names when they stand still.

**Against:**
- Behaviours wander. The Gulf Stream meanders, polynyas open and close, bores only run at spring tides. A name attaches to *where the behaviour usually is*, not to a line.
- Some behaviours are periodic, so a chart needs a *when* as well as a *where*. Real charts already do this: tidal-stream tables give set and rate by hours before and after high water.

**Revised:** keep the premise as the module's central rule. Behaviour-features carry a 'usually here' sense in the guide text, and periodic ones (tides, bores, slacks) are described as times, not lines.

### 1.2 'Sort terms by terrain, route, condition or infrastructure' (critique)

**For:** the right instinct. The lexicon flattens refuge, route, phase and landform into one list.

**Against:**
- Four bins leak. A **race** is a condition and a place name at once: the *Race of Alderney* is the name of a strait.
- Not every refuge is infrastructure. A roadstead is *defined* by natural shelter; **Poole Harbour**, **Chichester Harbour** and **Milford Haven** name natural bodies of water, not quays.
- Persistent motion needs its own bin: currents, races, gyres and eddies are neither terrain nor conditions.

**Revised:** five bins – **terrain**, **water** (persistent motion), **route**, **state**, **civic**. Natural refuges (lee, roads, anchorage, pool, hope, haven, natural harbour) are terrain; built things (port, berth, mooring, buoy, light, marker, beacon) are civic and stay with buildings and landmarks (which already has lighthouses and beacons) or with spacecraft and stations.

### 1.3 'Every chart records depth, current, density/purchase and strain/shear' (critique)

**For:**
- Depth and current are exactly what real charts record: soundings, and tidal diamonds giving **set** (direction) and **drift** (rate).
- *Purchase* has a real counterpart the critique doesn't spot: **holding ground**. Charts mark the bottom (sand, mud, clay, rock) because it decides whether an anchor holds. 'Good holding' is a sailor's phrase already.

**Against:**
- Shear isn't charted. It is derived from neighbouring currents.
- Real charts also record **hazards** and **magnetic variation** – the gap between where the compass says north is and where it is. The hyperspace equivalent (instruments disagreeing with the medium) is missing, and it is a rich naming source: *False Bearing*, *the Crooked Reach*, *Liar's Bank*.

**Revised:** a hyperspace chart records **depth**, **set and drift**, **holding** and **hazards**; shear and variation are notes. These live in the module's guide text and tooltips, not as data.

### 1.4 'Compressibility is a setting decision; a waterlike ocean doesn't make shocks' (critique)

**For:** in gas dynamics, shocks, rarefactions and expansion fans do need a compressible medium, and a setting should say which physics it uses.

**Against:**
- The second half is wrong for water with a surface. The **shallow-water equations** have the same mathematical form as two-dimensional compressible gas flow – the *hydraulic analogy*. A **hydraulic jump** behaves like a shock, the **Froude number** plays the part of the Mach number, and a **bore** (the Severn Bore, the Trent Aegir) is a moving hydraulic jump.
- Flow over a **sill** can go critical. Oceanographers call it **hydraulic control**: it governs the exchange through the Strait of Gibraltar, and in the Denmark Strait cold water spills over a sill in the largest known waterfall on Earth, entirely underwater.
- Both notes already give hyperspace a **depth**. A medium with depth and a critical wave speed produces the shock family for free, as bores, jumps, spills and overfalls.
- **Cavitation** is misfiled. It is a *liquid* phenomenon – vapour pockets where pressure drops, the thing that pits ship propellers. It belongs to the sea model, not the gas model.

**Revised:** offer the choice as one link in the hyperspace sentence – hyperspace flows **like the sea** (default: bores, jumps, spills, overfalls, boils, cavitation, dead water) or **like the wind** (shock, shock train, rarefaction, expansion fan, pressure ridge, squalls, doldrums). Shared words appear in both.

### 1.5 Attractors need somewhere for the flow to go (neither note)

Both notes use **attractor**, **draw**, **drift trap**, **convergence** and 'whirlpool with an inward draw'. In an incompressible flow, a current cannot converge on a surface unless the water goes somewhere – in the real ocean, convergence zones are **downwelling** and divergence zones are **upwelling**.

That turns a quiet inconsistency into a rule that makes the invented terms cohere:

- A **Draw** (attractor) is a downwelling: a descent into deeper hyperspace. On a chart it is a **Depthwell**.
- A **Spill** (repeller) is an upwelling: hyperspace welling up towards realspace. On a chart it is a **Depthrise**.
- A **drift trap** is a closed eddy over a well – you circle and sink.

**Revised:** all three registers keep their words, but the guide explains a draw as a well and a spill as a rise.

### 1.6 'Use three vocabularies: navigator speech, chart language, theoretical language' (critique)

**For:**
- Real naming has registers: fishermen's names, Admiralty chart names and oceanographers' names differ. The stellar review used the same split (folk and formal forms).
- It lets characters reveal background through word choice, as the critique's freighter-captain example shows.

**Against:**
- The critique's own sorting is off. **Overfalls** is printed on every chart *and* said by every sailor; **pycnocline** never appears on a navigational chart – it is pure survey language; **anchorage** is both speech and chart.
- A register is not a tone. nameForge's tones (grand, plain, grim, light, strange) are moods; registers are who is speaking. They need their own control.

**Revised:** a **register** link in hyperspace charts only (spacers' words, chart words, survey words, or any). The earthly modules mix speech and chart forms naturally, as real coasts do.

### 1.7 'Main is the navigators' word for hyperspace as a whole' (lexicon)

**For:** 'the main' is a real poetic word for the open sea ('the bounding main').

**Against:**
- The *Spanish Main* originally meant the mainland coast of the Caribbean, and only later the sea.
- In Orkney and Shetland, **Mainland** is the largest island.

**Revised:** Core for hyperspace at galaxy and sector scales, as a folk name for the whole medium. Never on small features (*the Vey Main* for a shoal would read wrongly).

### 1.8 The five-level naming hierarchy (lexicon)

**For:** it maps neatly onto nameForge's existing space scales: great regions at galaxy and sector, regional formations at sector, routes at neighbourhood, local formations and hazards at star system.

**Against:**
- Levels mix kinds: *gyre* sits in level I but *current* in level II.
- Level V includes **buoy**, **light** and **marker**, which are civic.

**Revised:** drop the levels; tag every term with the space scales it suits and let the existing scale link choose.

### 1.9 'A feature earns a place name when it is persistent, predictable and consequential' (critique)

**For:** an excellent filter, and the same test the stellar review used.

**Against:** too strict for real naming. Places are also named for a single memorable event or feeling: *Cape Disappointment*, *Desolation Sound*, *Poverty Bay*, *Cape Farewell*, *Turnagain Arm*, *Wreck Bay*. Explorers named coasts after moods and moments, not only after hazards.

**Revised:** persistent **or** memorable. The memorable route becomes an 'explorer's mood' word pool in the earthly modules.

### 1.10 'Section 1 can be handed directly to another agent' (lexicon)

**Against:** not yet. Section 1 mixes freshwater (*lake*, *mere*), river terms (*eyot*, *reach*), and infrastructure (*harbour*, *port*, *berth*); it has no regional or cultural dimension; and it never records **word order**, which decides whether a name sounds right (*Loch Ness* but *Ness Point*; *Firth of Forth* but *Moray Firth*; *Cape Wrath* but *Flamborough Head*).

**Revised:** the brief supplies order, region and culture; the review's verdicts supply the sorting.

### 2.12 The invented hyperspace terms

**A. Depth and dimensional access.** For: depth is the backbone, and these are the best words in the lexicon. Two have real nautical roots worth knowing: sailors spoke of the *hundred-fathom line* (so **Depthline** is an old idea in new clothes), and of being **in soundings** (within measurable depth) or **off soundings** (beyond it), which makes **Sounding field** and **Unsounded sea** authentic. Against: *fold* collides with *Dune*'s **foldspace**, but only as that one word. Revised: Depthline, Deepfold, Depthwell, Depthrise, Undershelf, Shallowfold Core or Chart · H; *False bottom* Core · H (strange); *Bottomless reach* Cut (melodrama).

**B. Reality boundaries.** For: **Realshore**, **Voidshore**, **Starshore**, **Riftmouth** are strong. **False shore** has a real model: *False Bay*, *False Point*, *False Cape* name features mistaken for others. Against: *Masscoast* is clunky; *Leeward reality* is a phrase, not a name; *Emergence beach* is long. Revised: Realshore, Starshore, Voidshore, Hard/Soft Coast, False Shore, Riftmouth Core · H; *Emergence beach* becomes plain **Beach**; Masscoast, Leeward reality Cut.

**C. Routes.** For: in hyperspace a current *is* a route, so the line blurs. Against: the stellar review already sent routes to route names. Revised: **natural** currents and threads are features (Flowroad, Threadway, Tidepath, Return path, Braided way, Ghost lane) · H; **maintained or regulated** ones (Current lane, Pilot's cut, Wakeway, Counterway) are Route, for a later link with route names' hyperspace lanes. *Braided* has a real model in braided rivers.

**D. Hazards.** For: the critique singles out Gravity Shoal and Reality Reef. Against: they are almost all one pattern – [physical quality] + [nautical feature] – which the lexicon's own §7 states. A generator should *build* them, not list them. **Dead current** has the real *dead water* behind it (§2.11E); **Shipwake storm** is weather. Revised: a {hyperPhysical} pool (Gravity, Mass, Phase, Time, Echo, Mirror, Causality) × hazard generics; *Drift trap* Core · H; *Blind tide* Core · H; storms to the weather belts.

**E. Calm and refuge.** For: **Stillwater**, **Slack basin**, **Hush**, **Restwater**, **Doldrums** (real) are good. Against: *Blue haven* is weak; *Mooring point* is civic; *Anchor field* is an anchorage; *Quiet deep* is a phrase. Revised: Stillwater, Slack Basin, Lee, Eye, Hush, Restwater, Deadwater, Still Point Core · H; Doldrums Core · H (wind medium); others Cut.

**F. Weather.** For: the lexicon's terrain-versus-weather split is right. Against: weather is only a *place* when it is persistent – the Roaring Forties are a place, a squall is not. Revised: persistent **weather belts** stay in hyperspace charts; transient storms go to events and eras later. One gift from the real sea: the North Sea's **Broad Fourteens** and **Long Forties** are named after their soundings in fathoms. Hyperspace depth bands can be named the same way (*the Howling Forties* for the fortieth band).

### 2.13 The lexicon's patterns and core vocabulary

**For:** [Person] + [Feature], [Possessive] + [Feature], [Colour] + [Feature], [Number] + [Feature] are all real.

**Against – missing patterns, all real and productive:**

- **Of-order versus fused order**, which varies by culture and region (*Sea of Azov* / *Black Sea*; *Bay of Biscay* / *Botany Bay*).
- **Gaelic and Welsh order** (generic first: *Loch Ness*, *Kyle of Lochalsh*, *Mull of Kintyre*, *Porthcawl*).
- **False X** (*False Bay*).
- **Explorer's mood** (*Poverty Bay*, *Bay of Plenty*, *Cape Disappointment*).
- **Resemblance** (Lake Biwa after the *biwa* lute; Erhai, 'Ear Sea', after its shape; the French *La Manche*, 'the Sleeve').
- **Personification** (*Old Man of Hoy*, *Old Harry Rocks*, *the Maidens*, *the Merry Men of Mey*).
- **Depth-number names** (*Broad Fourteens*, *Long Forties*).
- **Boats and ships** (*Dogger Bank* after Dutch *dogger* fishing boats; *Challenger Deep* and *Porcupine Bank* after survey ships).
- **Peoples** (Roman *Mare Germanicum*, the 'German Sea'; Arabic 'Sea of the Romans' for the Mediterranean).
- **Euphemism** (the Greek *Euxine*, 'hospitable', for a sea first called 'inhospitable').
- **Kennings** (Old English *hronrād*, 'whale-road', and *ganotes bæþ*, 'gannet's bath').
- **Colour seas** (Turkish *Akdeniz*, 'White Sea', for the Mediterranean and *Karadeniz*, 'Black Sea'; the idea that the colours are compass points is a hypothesis, not established).
- **Cosmological oceans** (the seven Puranic oceans: of salt, sugar-cane juice, wine, ghee, curd, milk and fresh water).
- **Lunar maria** (Riccioli's 1651 weather-and-mood names: *Sea of Tranquillity*, *Ocean of Storms*, *Lake of Dreams*).
- **Sea monsters** (Titan's seas are formally named after mythical sea creatures: *Kraken Mare*, *Ligeia Mare*, *Punga Mare*).

**[Religious or legendary] + [technical formation]** (*Saint Orra's Separatrix*): For: funny and alien. Against: the register clash is the joke, so it can't be common. Revised: strange tone only.

The **core vocabulary** list in the lexicon's §8 is sound, with *Separatrix* moved to chart words and *Shore* defined as the hyperspace emergence edge, not a coastline.

## 4. What both notes miss

1. **Word order by culture and region.** English has both orders even for one generic (*Firth of Forth*, *Moray Firth*); Gaelic and Welsh put the generic first; Arabic and Latin naming prefer 'Sea of …'; Chinese, Japanese and Korean prefer the generic last.
2. **British regional coastal words.** *Ness*, *naze*, *bill*, *mull*, *nab*, *ord*, *noup*, *geo*, *voe*, *wick*, *hope*, *holm*, *skerry*, *stack*, *clett*, *calf*, *inch*, *kyle*, *chine*, *pill*, *fleet*, *gat*, *swin*, *swatchway*, *knock*, *wash*, *scar*, *brigg*, *denes*, *links*, *machair*, *saltings*, *ayre*, *snook*, *tarn*, *broad*, *water* (for a lake). Each belongs to particular regions; the brief weights them.
3. **The naming conventions in §2.13** – explorer's mood, false, resemblance, personification, depth numbers, boats and survey ships, peoples, euphemism, kennings, colours, cosmological oceans, maria and sea monsters.
4. **Folk etymology and fossil names.** *Cape Wrath* is from Norse *hvarf*, 'turning point'; *Cape Horn* is from the town of Hoorn; Cornwall's *Manacles* are 'church stones' in Cornish; the *Bosporus* is 'ox ford'. This backs the stellar review's point that names fossilise and get reheard.
5. **Realistic supernatural names.** *Devil's Hole*, *Witch Ground*, *Giant's Causeway*, the *Devil's Frying Pan* and *Mermaid's Rock* are all real. Folk-supernatural names belong in realistic mode; only open monsters and magic need the fantastic switch.
6. **Biome.** Atolls, cays, lagoons and reef passes are tropical; fjords, skerries and polynyas are cold; bitter and salt lakes are dry; meres and broads are lowland. nameForge's twelve biomes already exist and can weight all of this.
7. **Overlap with what nameForge already has.**
   - Route names already has **sea routes** (*Passage*, *Sea Road*, *Run*) and the faster-than-light 'by hyperspace lanes' choice: routes stay there.
   - Buildings and landmarks already has **lighthouses and beacons**: lights stay there.
   - British place names already has **coast-and-sea settlement generics** (settlements *called* after coasts): the new modules name the features themselves.
   - The realspace space vocabulary already uses *Shoals, Drift, Reach, Deep, Well, Rim, Bar, Halo, Gap, Lens, Filament, Bubble, Sheet*: hyperspace charts must draw from their own pools, never from `spaceLand`, so a batch never mixes a realspace *Shoals* with a hyperspace one.

## 5. Collisions to safeguard

Single generic words stay; exact phrases are blocked.

**Hyperspace (franchise):** *The Maelstrom*, *Eye of Terror*, *the Immaterium*, *the Webway* (Warhammer 40,000); *Witch Space*, *Witchspace* (Elite); *Foldspace* (*Dune*); *Slow Zone* (Vinge and *The Expanse*), *the Unthinking Depths*, *the Transcend* (Vinge); *Infraspace*, *Ultraspace* (Banks); *Astral Sea*, *Wildspace*, *Phlogiston* (Spelljammer); *Hydian Way*, *Perlemian Trade Route*, *Corellian Run*, *Rimma Trade Route*, *the Maw* (Star Wars); plus nameForge's existing space blocks (*Kessel Run*, *Spinward Marches*, *The Expanse* and the rest). Also the Earth wind belts *Roaring Forties*, *Furious Fifties*, *Screaming Sixties*, which would read as earthly in a hyperspace batch.

**Earthly (franchise):** *Grey Havens* (Tolkien); *Sword Coast*, *Sea of Fallen Stars* (Forgotten Realms); *Isle of Dread* (D&D); *Sea of Thieves*, *Sea of Stars* (games); *Skull Island* (King Kong).

**Real-world coincidences** (*North Sea*, *Black Sea*, *Dogger Bank*, *Ivory Coast*): allowed, as british place names already allows *Oxford*. Directional and colour names are realistic precisely because they recur. The brief keeps the colour pool's weight modest so they don't dominate.

**Left out entirely:** *Slave Coast* and similar names built on atrocity.

## 7. What this means for nameForge

The two notes describe four different things, and they want four modules sharing one engine, in a new **seas and coasts** group:

| Module | Covers | Settings |
|---|---|---|
| **seas and lakes** | Oceans, seas, gulfs and bays, firths/fjords/sea lochs, straits and channels, lakes and lochs | Culture, era (incl. other worlds), home or new coast, region or biome, tone, fantastic |
| **coasts and islands** | Headlands, cliffs and rocks, beaches and sands, coves and inlets, islands, coasts and shores | As above, plus a 'stretch of coast' series |
| **sea charts** | Banks and shoals, reefs and ledges, deeps and holes, races and currents, roads and anchorages, channels and swatchways | As coasts |
| **hyperspace charts** | The lexicon's hyperspace terrain, revised as above | Scale, register, medium (sea or wind), culture, tone |

The brief sets out the data, weights and milestones.
